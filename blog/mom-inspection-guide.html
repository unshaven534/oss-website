What actually happens during a MOM workplace inspection — and how to not be caught off guard.

A plain-English guide to what inspectors look for, what they can issue on the spot, and how to prepare so that an inspection is a non-event rather than a crisis.

By OSS → 6 min read → Compliance
MOM Inspection
What to expect — Singapore SMEs

Most Singapore SME owners have never been through a MOM inspection. So when an inspector shows up — and they will, eventually — the instinct is to panic. That panic is usually what makes things worse.

Here’s what the process actually looks like, what inspectors are there to do, and how you can prepare so that an inspection is a non-event rather than a crisis.

1. Who conducts the inspections

MOM’s Occupational Safety and Health Division (OSHD) is responsible for workplace inspections across Singapore. Inspectors are authorised under the Workplace Safety and Health Act to enter any workplace at any time without advance notice. They can examine documents, interview workers, take photographs, and issue notices on the spot.

They are not there to catch you out. They are there to verify that you have a functioning safety management system — and if you don’t, to tell you to fix it.

2. What triggers an inspection

Inspections are not random, though some routine ones are. The most common triggers are:

  • A reported workplace accident or dangerous occurrence. If someone gets hurt and it’s reported to MOM (as required), an inspector will typically follow up.
  • A complaint from a worker. MOM takes these seriously and does not disclose the identity of the person who filed the complaint.
  • An industry sweep. MOM periodically targets specific sectors — F&B, construction, warehousing — where injury rates are higher.
  • A follow-up from a previous notice. If you’ve received an improvement notice before, expect a revisit to check compliance.

3. What happens when they arrive

The inspector will identify themselves and state the purpose of the visit. From that point, you are legally required to cooperate — including providing access to your premises and your documentation.

They will typically ask to see:

  • Your WSH Policy
  • Your Risk Assessments (RAs)
  • Safe Work Procedures (SWPs) for high-risk tasks
  • Incident and near-miss records
  • Training records for your workers
  • Your first aid provisions and emergency response plan

They may also walk the premises, speak directly to workers (without management present), and observe work being done.

Commonly missed: Owners sometimes try to delay or “prepare” before letting the inspector in. This is not advisable. Inspectors note the delay, and a scramble to find paperwork signals that the system isn’t working day-to-day — which is exactly what they’re looking for.

4. What they can issue on the spot

If an inspector identifies a hazard or non-compliance, they have several tools available immediately:

  • An Improvement Notice (IN) requires you to fix a specific issue by a specific date. It is not a fine — it is an instruction. Most first-time issues result in an IN.
  • A Stop-Work Order (SWO) halts specific operations or the entire workplace until the hazard is rectified. These are reserved for immediate and serious risks. The financial cost of a SWO — in lost revenue, idle staff wages, and reputational damage — is typically far higher than any fine.
  • Prosecution is reserved for serious breaches, repeat offenders, or cases involving injury. Under the WSH Act, individuals (including directors) can be personally fined or imprisoned.

Commonly missed: Owners treat an Improvement Notice as a pass. It isn’t. An IN goes on your record, and a second visit that finds the same issue unresolved will be treated much more seriously.

5. What to do while the inspector is on site

Stay calm. Assign one person — ideally your WSH Officer or the most senior person present — to accompany the inspector throughout.

Answer questions honestly. Inspectors are experienced at spotting inconsistencies, and an attempt to mislead them is a separate offence under the WSH Act.

Take notes. Write down every observation the inspector makes and every document they request. This becomes your action list if a notice is issued.

Do not instruct workers on what to say. Inspectors routinely speak to workers separately, and coaching workers to give particular answers is both obvious and a serious compliance risk.

Commonly missed: Nobody has been designated to handle an inspection. On the day, there’s confusion about who should speak, where the documents are, and what authority the inspector has. This is entirely preventable with thirty minutes of preparation.

6. After the inspection

If no notice is issued, you’re done — but use the experience. Review what the inspector looked at, and plug any gaps you noticed in your own system during the visit.

If an Improvement Notice is issued, read it carefully. The notice will specify exactly what needs to be fixed and by when. Respond before the deadline — not the day before. MOM tracks compliance, and early, documented action demonstrates good faith.

If a Stop-Work Order is issued, get professional help immediately. The cost of staying stopped is significant, and you need someone who knows how to engage MOM on a remediation plan.

An inspection is not a test you can cram for. The businesses that sail through them aren’t the ones who prepared the week before — they’re the ones who have a maintained, functioning safety system as part of how they operate every day.
