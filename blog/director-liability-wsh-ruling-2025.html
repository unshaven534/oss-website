High Court clarifies director liability in landmark WSH ruling — what every Singapore business owner must know.

PP v Yeo Teck Soon [2025] SGHC 179 establishes a two-stage sentencing framework for officer liability under the WSH Act. Delegation is no longer a defence.

By OSS → 10 min read → Legal Update → January 2026
S$150,000
Director Liability — WSH Act Singapore 2025

In September 2025, Singapore’s High Court delivered a judgment that every company director, board member, and senior manager in the construction, marine, and manufacturing sectors needs to read. Public Prosecutor v Yeo Teck Soon [2025] SGHC 179 establishes — for the first time — a clear, principled sentencing framework for officer liability under the Workplace Safety and Health Act (WSHA).

The ruling signals a decisive shift toward individual accountability. A director who delegates safety responsibilities but fails to oversee them is legally negligent. The era of hiding behind “I didn’t know” is over.

Delegation without verification is not a defence. It is an admission of failure.

The incident: corroded formwork at Jalan Buroh

The case originated from a July 2016 construction site incident in Singapore. Workers were installing table formwork when a section collapsed, striking a worker and causing serious injuries. MOM investigators found that the formwork components — including wedges and stacking pins — were severely corroded. More troubling: the defects had been painted over, apparently to conceal the deterioration.1

The formwork had been supplied by Formwork Hire (S.E.A.) Pte Ltd. Prosecutors charged not just the company, but its director personally: Mr. Yeo Teck Soon.

The charges: Section 16 and Section 48 of the WSHA

Mr. Yeo was charged under two provisions operating in tandem:

  • Section 16(1)(b) WSHA — imposes a duty on suppliers of machinery and equipment used at work to ensure that equipment is safe and without health risks when properly used2
  • Section 48(1) WSHA — the officer liability provision: where a corporate entity commits an offence, any officer who consented, connived, or neglected to prevent the offence is guilty of the same offence and liable to the same punishment3

The prosecution argued that Mr. Yeo had neglected to implement adequate systems to ensure formwork components were inspected, maintained, and certified safe before being supplied to worksites.

The High Court’s ruling: conviction upheld

Chief Justice Sundaresh Menon upheld Mr. Yeo’s conviction and made clear that neglect under Section 48(1) does not require proof that the director personally knew of the specific hazard. What matters is whether the director failed to implement reasonable systems of oversight.5

The Court identified several failures on Mr. Yeo’s part:

  • No documented system for inspecting formwork components before supply
  • No training given to staff on corrosion detection
  • No audit trail showing the director had ever reviewed inventory condition
  • Defects painted over — a culture of concealment a diligent director should have detected

The Court also rejected the “delegation defence” outright. A director can delegate tasks, but cannot delegate responsibility. Appointing a safety officer and never reviewing their reports is still negligence if a breach occurs.

The two-stage sentencing framework

Having affirmed liability, Chief Justice Menon established a binding two-stage framework for all future officer liability cases under the WSHA.6

Stage 1: Indicative sentence based on harm and culpability

The court assesses harm level (Low / Medium / High) and culpability level (Low / Medium / High) using a matrix:

  • High harm — death, permanent disability, or life-threatening injury
  • Medium harm — serious injury requiring hospitalisation or resulting in long-term impairment
  • Low harm — minor injury requiring little or no medical treatment
  • High culpability — knowingly disregarded safety rules, encouraged unsafe practices, systematically cut corners for profit
  • Medium culpability — aware of risks but took inadequate or sporadic action
  • Low culpability — reasonable effort but fell short due to oversight, without reckless disregard

In Mr. Yeo’s case: Medium harm + Low culpability = indicative sentence of S$120,000.

Stage 2: Adjust for offender-specific factors

  • Aggravating — prior convictions, concealment, lack of remorse, repeat offending
  • Mitigating — timely plea of guilt, cooperation with MOM, remedial actions, good corporate citizenship

Mr. Yeo had cooperated with MOM, expressed remorse, and improved safety systems post-incident. Final sentence: S$150,000. The prosecution’s appeal for a higher fine was dismissed.

Custodial sentences remain on the table. For high harm and high culpability — such as a director who knowingly disregards safety leading to a fatality — imprisonment is explicitly contemplated.

Key legal principles now binding on all directors

  • Neglect does not require knowledge — you can be liable even without knowing about the specific breach, if you failed to build systems that would have caught it
  • Delegation is not a defence — hiring a WSHO or safety team without actively overseeing them is still negligence
  • The framework applies to all officers — directors, managers, secretaries, and equivalents charged under Section 48(1)
  • Imprisonment remains possible — for high harm and high culpability, jail is explicitly on the table

How this compares to other 2025 cases

The Yeo Teck Soon ruling is part of a broader trend. In PP v Ong Bok Wan (2025), a director received 30 weeks’ imprisonment — the key factor being personal negligence leading directly to a fatality (High harm). In PP v ST Engineering Marine (2025), the corporate entity was fined S$400,000 as a repeat offender.9 The Yeo Teck Soon framework applies equally across all three outcomes.

What directors should do now

Following this ruling, OSS recommends every director in a regulated industry take these steps:

  1. Document your safety delegation clearly — a board-approved org chart showing who owns each WSH obligation, updated annually
  2. Establish a board-level safety review — quarterly meetings reviewing incident rates, near-misses, MOM findings, and audit results, with written minutes
  3. Require written WSHO reports — your registered WSHO should provide written reports to a designated director, who notes their review in writing
  4. Complete MOM-recognised director safety training — the WSH for Business Owners and Senior Management programme provides documented evidence of due diligence
  5. Commission independent audits — third-party audits show active engagement, not box-ticking
  6. Maintain a due diligence file — board minutes, safety committee reports, risk assessments, audit findings, training certificates, and MOM correspondence in one accessible file
