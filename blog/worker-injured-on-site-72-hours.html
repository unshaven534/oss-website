Incident Response

Your worker got injured on site. Here’s exactly what happens and what to do in the next 72 hours.

The minutes and days after a workplace injury are the most consequential of your compliance life. What you do — and what you fail to do — determines your legal exposure, your WICA liability, and whether MOM treats you as a responsible employer or a prosecution target.

By OSS → 9 min read → Incident Response

First: understand what you are dealing with

Not all workplace injuries carry the same obligations. Before anything else, classify the incident correctly. Under the Work Injury Compensation Act (WICA) and the WSH (Incident Reporting) Regulations, there are four categories you need to know:

Classification Definition Reporting Deadline
Dangerous Occurrence Structural collapse, explosion, crane failure, or other specified events — regardless of injury Immediately by phone, then within 10 days via iReport
Fatality Death of any person at or arising from the workplace Immediately by phone to MOM, within 10 days via iReport
Major Injury Fracture (excluding finger/toe), amputation, loss of sight, burns covering >10% body, hospitalisation >24 hours Within 10 days via iReport
Minor Injury / MC Any injury resulting in medical leave (MC) or light duty of 3 or more consecutive days (including day of accident) Within 10 days via iReport

If you are unsure which category applies, err on the side of treating it as more serious. The consequences of under-reporting are far worse than over-reporting.

Failing to report a workplace injury within the required timeframe is itself an offence under the WSH Act, separate from whatever caused the accident. You can be penalised twice — once for the unsafe condition and once for the cover-up.

The first hour: stabilise and preserve

Your immediate priority is your worker. Get them medical attention. If the injury is serious, call 995. Do not move an injured person unnecessarily unless they are in immediate danger from a secondary hazard such as fire or structural collapse.

Once emergency services are on the way or the worker is receiving care, do the following before anything else disturbs the scene:

  • Secure the area. Cordon off the scene. Do not allow anyone to clean up, move equipment, or resume work in the immediate vicinity until a proper investigation has been done. This is not bureaucracy — it is evidence preservation, and it protects you.
  • Take photographs immediately. Document the scene exactly as it was found — equipment positions, materials, spills, lighting, access routes, PPE worn by the worker. Photograph from multiple angles. Use your phone. Do this now, before anyone tidies anything.
  • Identify witnesses. Get the names and contact details of everyone who saw what happened or was working nearby. Do this before shifts end and people go home.
  • Preserve physical evidence. The tool, machine, harness, or substance involved in the accident must not be removed, repaired, or modified until MOM gives clearance. If a machine malfunctioned, tag it out of service and lock it.
  • Notify your management chain immediately. Your supervisor, operations manager, and if you have one, your WSHO or safety officer, must be informed now — not at the end of the shift.

Hours 1–24: notify and document

Notify MOM via iReport

For fatalities and dangerous occurrences, call MOM immediately on 1800-221-9922 (WSH hotline). For all other reportable incidents, you have up to 10 days — but do not wait. Log in to iReport at mom.gov.sg and submit the incident report as soon as the facts are established.

iReport will ask for:

  • Worker’s personal details, work pass number, and employment status
  • Date, time, and location of the accident
  • Nature of the injury and body part affected
  • Description of what happened and the immediate cause
  • Whether the worker has been seen by a doctor and the estimated days of MC

Your WSHO or a senior manager should submit this. Do not delegate it to someone unfamiliar with the incident facts — inaccuracies in the initial report create problems later.

Notify your insurer

If your worker is covered under WICA (Work Injury Compensation Act), notify your Work Injury Compensation insurer immediately. Under the Act, employers must have WIC insurance for all employees doing manual work and all employees earning S$2,600 or less per month, regardless of job nature. Delay in notifying your insurer can complicate the claims process.

Your insurer will typically need the same details as iReport — worker details, incident description, medical report, and MC duration. Keep your policy number accessible.

Begin your incident record

Open an incident file. This is not optional and not just for MOM — it is your protection. The file should contain:

  • Incident report form (internal)
  • All photographs taken at the scene
  • Witness statements (written, signed, and dated)
  • Medical certificates and doctor’s reports as they come in
  • Equipment inspection records or maintenance logs for any plant involved
  • The worker’s risk assessment for the task being performed at the time
  • Any permit-to-work or safe work procedure relevant to the activity

Hours 24–72: investigate properly

This is where most employers fail. They treat the incident report as the end of their obligation. It is not. It is the beginning.

Within 72 hours, you should have completed or be well into a formal incident investigation. The purpose is not to assign blame — it is to identify the root cause so the same thing cannot happen again. MOM inspectors know the difference between a genuine investigation and a box-ticking exercise, and they will ask to see your findings.

Conduct the investigation

A proper workplace incident investigation looks at three levels of cause:

  • Immediate cause: What directly caused the injury? Slipped on wet floor, caught hand in unguarded machinery, fell from height without harness.
  • Underlying cause: Why did the immediate cause exist? No anti-slip mat, machine guard removed for maintenance and not replaced, harness not provided or worker not trained to use it.
  • Root cause: What systemic failure allowed the underlying cause to persist? No hazard identification process, no pre-task inspection, no supervisory checks, no safety induction for new workers.

Interview the injured worker if they are able. Interview all witnesses separately. Review the relevant risk assessment — was this hazard identified? Were controls in place? Were they being followed? If the answer to any of these is no, that is a finding.

Implement immediate corrective actions

Do not wait for the investigation to be fully written up before acting. If the hazard that caused the injury still exists, fix it now. Temporary controls are acceptable while permanent fixes are arranged, but document what you have put in place and why.

MOM inspectors who visit after a reportable incident will look specifically at whether you have taken interim measures to prevent recurrence. If the scene is unchanged and the same hazard is still present, you are looking at an improvement notice or stop-work order.

What MOM will do next

For serious injuries, dangerous occurrences, and fatalities, expect MOM to visit. For minor injuries with MC, they may or may not attend — but they will review your iReport submission and may follow up by phone or letter.

When MOM inspectors arrive:

  • They will want to see the accident scene and may take their own photographs
  • They will ask to interview the supervisor on duty, the injured worker’s colleagues, and your management representative
  • They will request all documentation: risk assessments, toolbox talk records, training records, maintenance logs, permits
  • They may issue an Improvement Notice requiring specific corrective actions by a set date
  • In serious cases or where there is evidence of gross negligence or wilful non-compliance, they can issue a Stop-Work Order (SWO) halting all or part of your operations
  • In the most serious cases, they will refer the matter to the Attorney-General’s Chambers for potential prosecution under the WSH Act

Cooperate fully. Do not destroy or alter evidence. Do not coach workers on what to say. Inspectors have seen every version of this — obstruction makes outcomes dramatically worse.

WICA: what your worker is entitled to

Separately from MOM’s enforcement action, your injured worker has rights under the Work Injury Compensation Act. WICA provides a no-fault compensation system — the worker does not need to prove negligence to claim. As the employer, you are responsible for ensuring they receive what they are entitled to.

WICA Benefit Entitlement
Medical expenses Up to S$45,000 or 1 year from date of accident, whichever comes first. Covers hospitalisation, surgery, physiotherapy, and follow-up treatment.
Temporary incapacity (MC pay) For the duration of certified medical leave, you must continue to pay the worker’s salary. This applies even if they are on extended MC for months.
Permanent incapacity lump sum Compensation based on the degree of permanent disability, capped at S$289,000 for total permanent incapacity. Calculated as a percentage of the maximum based on the affected body part.
Death compensation Lump sum paid to dependants, capped at S$225,000 (or S$57,000 for workers aged 65 and above). Funeral expenses of up to S$9,000 also covered.

Your WIC insurer handles the actual payment of claims, but as the employer you remain the legally responsible party. If you do not have the required insurance in place, you bear the liability personally.

The documentation that protects you

In the aftermath of a workplace injury, your documentation is your defence. If MOM investigates and your records are clean, current, and complete, you demonstrate that you operate a genuine safety management system — not just one on paper. If they are incomplete, outdated, or non-existent, every subsequent finding is compounded.

The documents that matter most after an incident:

  • Risk assessment for the task — was it done, was it current, was it communicated to the worker?
  • Safe Work Procedure (SWP) — was there one, did the worker follow it, was it adequate?
  • Training records — was the worker trained and inducted for the specific task and hazards involved?
  • Toolbox talk records — were safety briefings conducted? Was the relevant hazard covered recently?
  • Equipment inspection and maintenance logs — especially if plant or machinery was involved
  • Supervision records — who was the supervisor on duty and what were their responsibilities?
  • Permit-to-Work records — for high-risk activities, was the permit properly issued, signed, and closed?

If any of these documents show a gap, do not falsify them. Fabricating records after an incident is a criminal offence that turns a WSH compliance matter into something far more serious. Instead, acknowledge the gap in your corrective action plan and demonstrate what you are doing to address it.

Common mistakes employers make — and their consequences

  • Delaying the iReport. The 10-day clock starts from the date of the accident, not from when you receive the full medical report. Many employers wait for the MC to be finalised before reporting. That is wrong and may result in a late reporting offence.
  • Cleaning the scene before it is documented. Pressure to resume operations is understandable. But destroying evidence — even unintentionally — compromises your investigation and looks like concealment to an inspector.
  • Not telling the worker about WICA. Employers are required to inform injured workers of their right to claim under WICA. Failure to do so is an offence, and workers who are not informed sometimes pursue civil litigation instead, which is more expensive and unpredictable for you.
  • Pressuring workers not to claim. This happens. It is illegal. Workers who are pressured or threatened in connection with a WICA claim have recourse to MOM, and the resulting investigation will be far more damaging than the original claim.
  • Treating the investigation as a formality. A one-paragraph incident report that says “worker was not careful” is not an investigation. Attributing the cause entirely to worker negligence without examining the systemic conditions that allowed it is the single most common failure MOM sees — and the one most likely to trigger deeper scrutiny.

After the 72 hours: what comes next

The immediate crisis period is over, but your obligations continue. In the weeks following the incident:

  • Track the worker’s medical progress and maintain contact. You are still their employer and they are still entitled to salary during MC.
  • Complete your formal incident investigation report and circulate findings to management.
  • Implement all corrective actions from the investigation and record their completion.
  • Review and update the relevant risk assessment to reflect the new understanding of the hazard.
  • Conduct a toolbox talk with the team to share learnings from the incident — without assigning personal blame to the injured worker.
  • If MOM issued an Improvement Notice, ensure all items are closed by the stipulated deadline and keep evidence of closure.
  • Review whether the same hazard exists at other workstations, sites, or tasks and extend your corrective actions accordingly.
An incident is either a turning point or a warning ignored. How you respond in the 72 hours after is the clearest signal to MOM, your workers, and your own management of which one it will be.

When you need professional support immediately

If the injury is serious, if MOM has been notified, or if you are facing an inspection, you should have a MOM-registered WSHO involved from the start. A qualified WSHO can:

  • Lead the incident investigation to the standard MOM expects
  • Prepare and submit your iReport accurately
  • Liaise with MOM inspectors on your behalf
  • Review and update your risk assessments and corrective action plan
  • Advise you on what to say, what to preserve, and what to fix first

If your business does not currently have a WSHO engaged, this is the moment you wish it did. Getting one involved after an incident is still valuable — but having one before means the documentation is already in order, the risk assessments are current, and you are not starting from zero when MOM asks questions.
