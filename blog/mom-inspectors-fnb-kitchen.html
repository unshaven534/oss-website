What MOM inspectors look for in an F&B kitchen.

When inspectors walk through your kitchen door, they are not checking uniforms. They are running a systematic audit of your WSH practices, documentation, and employment compliance. Here is exactly what they look for — and how to be ready.

By OSS → 8 min read → F&B Compliance

The enforcement reality: inspections are intensifying

MOM conducts regular inspections of commercial and industrial workplaces to check compliance with employment regulations and workplace safety standards. From April 2023 to March 2024, MOM inspected over 4,100 retail and food service firms. In a single enforcement operation at Clarke Quay and Little India in July 2023, 20 out of 35 F&B establishments inspected were found to have infringed the Employment of Foreign Manpower Act.

That level of scrutiny is not letting up. If anything, it is increasing. Understanding exactly what inspectors look for could mean the difference between a clean bill of health and a costly fine — or worse, a stop-work order.

When MOM inspectors knock on your door, you want to be ready — not just to pass the inspection, but to protect your workers, your business, and your reputation.

1. Risk assessment documentation

The first thing inspectors will ask for is your risk assessment. Under the WSH Act, every F&B employer must conduct risk assessments for all work activities. This is not optional — failure to have a proper risk assessment is itself an offence.

Inspectors will check whether you have:

  • Identified all workplace hazards — hot oil, wet floors, sharp knives, machinery
  • Implemented appropriate control measures for each hazard
  • Documented the assessment properly and kept it on file
  • Reviewed and updated it regularly (at least every three years, or after any significant change)
  • Communicated the findings to the workers who carry out those tasks

2. Machinery and equipment safety

Since 1 January 2025, the WSH Act's Fifth Schedule expanded to include additional categories of high-risk machinery. Many common F&B kitchen appliances now fall under stricter regulatory oversight, including slicing machines, grinders, mincers, blenders, juicers, and mixers.

Inspectors will check that:

  • Machines have proper guards in place over moving parts
  • Emergency stop buttons are accessible and functional
  • Equipment is properly maintained and inspected on a documented schedule
  • Warning signage is displayed on equipment
  • Staff are trained in safe work procedures for each machine

3. Slip, trip, and fall prevention

Slips, trips, and falls account for close to 30% of all workplace injuries in Singapore, affecting more than 3,000 employees annually. In F&B, the risk is amplified by wet kitchen floors, grease, and high foot traffic near cooking stations.

Inspectors will look for:

  • Anti-slip flooring and mats in kitchen and food prep areas
  • Immediate spillage cleaning procedures with wet-floor signage in use
  • Non-slip footwear worn by all kitchen staff
  • Adequate lighting throughout the kitchen
  • Clear, unobstructed walkways between stations

4. Chemical safety and storage

Cleaning agents, sanitisers, and degreasers are present in every F&B kitchen. Inspectors will verify that:

  • Chemicals are stored in approved containers — never in drink or food containers
  • Chemical storage is segregated from food preparation and storage areas
  • Safety Data Sheets (SDS) are accessible for every hazardous chemical on site
  • Workers handling chemicals have been briefed on safe handling and PPE requirements

5. Electrical safety

Kitchens run high-powered equipment in wet, greasy conditions — a combination that creates serious electrical hazards. Inspectors examine:

  • Electrical equipment for signs of faulty wiring, damage, or deterioration
  • Proper isolation of equipment from power when not in use or during cleaning
  • Overloaded circuits and correct electrical load management
  • Ground fault protection in wet kitchen areas

6. Personal protective equipment (PPE)

Inspectors verify that appropriate PPE is provided and actually being used — not just available in a cupboard somewhere. For F&B kitchens, this means:

  • Heatproof gloves for handling hot cookware
  • Aprons providing burn and splash protection
  • Closed-toe, non-slip shoes for all kitchen staff
  • Cut-resistant gloves at knife and slicer stations

7. Safe work procedures and staff training

Inspectors will ask your staff direct questions to check whether they understand their safety obligations. They will also check documentation. Specifically, they want to see:

  • Written Safe Work Procedures for high-risk tasks, posted at the point of use
  • Records showing that workers were trained on those procedures
  • Evidence that new hires and part-time workers received safety inductions
  • Staff who can articulate emergency procedures without being prompted

A Safe Work Procedure filed in a binder that no one has read does not satisfy this requirement. The document must be a functioning safety control, not an administrative artefact.

8. Combustible dust handling

Since 1 January 2025, combustible dusts — including flour, sugar, and potato starch — have been brought under stricter regulation. This directly affects bakeries, central kitchens, and any F&B business handling bulk dry ingredients.

Inspectors will check that packages of 25 kg or more are properly labelled with hazard and safety information, and that bulk handling areas have adequate ventilation and dust control measures in place.

9. Employment compliance

MOM inspectors are equally focused on employment regulations alongside WSH. They will verify:

  • All foreign employees hold valid, current work passes
  • Workers are not illegally deployed to work for any other persons or businesses
  • Salaries are paid on time and in full
  • Migrant domestic workers are not deployed in F&B operations

From 2022 to 2024, MOM investigated 19 cases involving migrant domestic workers or foreigners working illegally in home-based F&B businesses. Employers who illegally deploy MDWs face fines of up to $10,000 and may be permanently barred from employing domestic workers.

10. Incident reporting and records

Inspectors will check that your incident records are complete and that statutory reporting obligations have been met. Under the WSH (Incident Reporting) Regulations, you are required to report:

  • A workplace fatality — within 10 days
  • An accident causing hospitalisation — within 10 days
  • An accident causing more than 3 consecutive days of medical leave — within 10 days
  • Dangerous occurrences with serious potential — as prescribed

Beyond statutory reporting, you should maintain an internal register of all incidents and near-misses — including minor ones. Near-miss recording is one of the first things a thorough inspector asks about, and the absence of any near-miss reports is itself a red flag.

The price of non-compliance

Penalties have increased substantially in recent years. Since June 2022, the composition fine for each WSH offence has doubled to a minimum of $2,000. For employment violations, employers who hire foreign employees without valid work passes face fines between $5,000 and $30,000, or up to 12 months' imprisonment, or both — per charge — plus a bar from employing foreign workers.

Recent enforcement actions give a sense of the exposure: in 2025, a commercial food service provider was fined $90,000 over safety lapses. One inspection sweep found more than half of the establishments visited to be in breach.

How to prepare before an inspector arrives

  • Review your risk assessment. Is it current? Does it cover your actual operations today, not the ones you had three years ago?
  • Check all machinery. Are guards in place? Are emergency stops functional and labelled?
  • Walk your floors. Are they clean and dry? Are anti-slip mats in place and in good condition?
  • Verify work passes. Are all foreign employees properly documented with current, valid passes?
  • Brief your staff. Can your kitchen workers describe their safe work procedures and emergency response steps without being coached?
  • Check chemical storage. Are all chemicals properly labelled and segregated from food areas?
  • Review your incident register. Are all accidents and near-misses recorded? Is statutory reporting up to date?
