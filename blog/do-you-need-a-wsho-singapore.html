Do you actually need a Workplace Safety and Health Officer in Singapore?

The legal triggers, the penalties for getting it wrong, and the part-time WSHO option most SME owners don’t know exists.

By OSS → 8 min read → WSH Regulations
WSHO
MOM Requirements — Singapore SMEs

In the first half of 2025 alone, Singapore’s Ministry of Manpower issued almost S$1.5 million in fines across nearly 7,000 breaches of workplace safety laws.1 For business owners and operations managers, this raises a critical question: does my company need a registered Workplace Safety and Health Officer?

The answer is not a simple yes or no. Under the Workplace Safety and Health (WSH) Act, the requirement to appoint a WSHO depends on your industry, the number of employees, and the nature of the work being conducted.

The question isn’t just whether the law requires one. It’s whether your business can afford not to have one.

When is a WSHO legally required?

The obligation is primarily triggered by high-risk activities and workforce thresholds. Under the WSH (General Provisions) Regulations and subsidiary legislation, a workplace must appoint a WSHO when:

  • Construction worksites — any project with a contract sum of S$10 million or more, or where 50 or more workers are employed2
  • Shipyards — any shipyard employing 50 or more workers3
  • Factories — manufacturing plants or industrial premises employing 100 or more workers4, including high-risk facilities involved in wafer fabrication, pharmaceutical production, or metalworking
  • Commissioner’s direction — even if your workplace doesn’t meet the above thresholds, MOM’s Commissioner has authority to direct the appointment of a WSHO if the nature of the work poses specific risks5

If your business falls under these categories, a WSHO is a mandatory statutory requirement — not optional.

The part-time WSHO option

The WSH Act recognises that not every business requires a full-time, on-site officer. Companies that need to meet regulatory standards but lack the volume of work for a full-time hire can engage a Part-Time WSHO.6 These professionals visit the workplace on scheduled intervals — weekly or monthly — to conduct inspections, review risk assessments, and ensure compliance.

This is a cost-effective strategy, and one that OSS routinely helps SMEs implement without the overhead of a permanent payroll. It’s also the model behind our OSS Overwatch subscription — a MOM-registered WSHO formally appointed to your business, for a fraction of a full-time salary.

What does a WSHO actually do?

A registered WSHO serves as your company’s primary guardian against safety failures. Their responsibilities extend well beyond checking boxes:

  • Regulatory compliance — ensuring adherence to the WSH Act and its 20+ subsidiary regulations, including those covering confined spaces, work at heights, and noise7
  • Risk management — developing, implementing, and maintaining Risk Assessments (RA) and Safe Work Procedures (SWP), reviewed at least once every three years8
  • Incident management — investigating accidents, dangerous occurrences, and near-misses, and submitting mandatory reports to MOM within 10 days9
  • Auditing and inspection — conducting regular site safety inspections and acting as secretary for WSH Committee meetings10
  • Training and communication — developing safety briefings, conducting toolbox meetings, and ensuring workers understand hazards through proper signage and permits-to-work11

The consequences of not having one

Failing to appoint a WSHO when required — or failing to perform the expected duties — exposes your company to severe penalties. Singapore has taken a hard line on safety lapses.

In 2024, MOM conducted over 17,000 inspections, detecting more than 16,000 breaches. Composition fines totalled S$3.1 million, alongside 58 Stop-Work Orders issued to halt dangerous operations.12 A Stop-Work Order can shut down your site for weeks, causing project delays and losses far beyond the fine itself.

The most breached provision of the WSH Act — Section 12(1) — mandates that employers take necessary measures to ensure the safety and health of their employees.13 Lacking a qualified WSHO to conduct risk assessments or maintain a safety management system is viewed by the courts as a failure to meet this standard.

A Stop-Work Order costs far more than a monthly safety subscription. Every time.

How to qualify as a registered WSHO

Registration with MOM is mandatory — you cannot give someone the title without government approval. Requirements include:

  • Age and status — at least 21 years old; open to Singaporeans, PRs, and valid EP/S Pass holders whose occupation title matches the approved list14
  • Qualifications — a prescribed WSH-related qualification such as the Specialist Diploma in WSH (via polytechnics or SISO), a BSc in WSH from SUSS, or a Master’s from NUS15
  • Experience — a minimum of two years of relevant work experience as an Assistant WSHO or Safety Coordinator, supported by documentary proof16

Once registered, the WSHO must renew their licence every two years and maintain credentials through Continuing Professional Development (CPD).17

Strategic advantages beyond compliance

Leading companies view the WSHO as a strategic asset, not just a regulatory requirement. In sectors like healthcare and logistics, WSHOs are increasingly integrated with Fire Safety Manager roles to manage complex hazards such as medical gas systems or infection control.18

WSHOs are also the designated competent persons for specialised tasks including gap analyses for noise control and chemical hazard management.19 Their expertise helps prevent the slips, trips, falls, and machinery accidents that constitute the bulk of MOM’s annual fines.20

The bottom line

If you operate a construction site with a S$10 million contract, a shipyard, or a factory with 100 or more workers — yes, a WSHO is the law. Failure to comply risks fines, Stop-Work Orders, and potential jail time for directors under the WSH Act.

For smaller operations, while not strictly mandatory, a part-time WSHO is a highly recommended risk management strategy. With Singapore recording thousands of breaches annually, the cost of a safety consultant is minimal compared to the financial impact of an accident or regulatory shutdown.
