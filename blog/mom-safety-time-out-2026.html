    MOM's Safety Time-Out ends July 31 — is your business actually ready?

    Higher fines, longer stop-work orders, and hiring bans are already in effect. Here's what changed, and what to check before the window closes.

    By OSS • 6 min read • Enforcement Update
    26 Jun – 31 Jul
    MOM Enhanced Enforcement Window

    If you run a business in Singapore with staff on the ground — a kitchen, a warehouse, a workshop, a vessel — there's a compliance window closing in a few days that you need to know about, even if nobody's mentioned it to you yet.

    What actually happened

    In late June 2026, seven workers lost their lives in five separate workplace accidents within a single month. That brought Singapore's 2026 workplace fatality count to 21, up from 18 over the same period last year. The incidents spanned different sectors and circumstances — MOM was clear there's no single cause tying them together, which is arguably the more worrying signal. It means the risk isn't isolated to one industry doing something wrong. It's spread out.

    In response, MOM didn't just issue a statement. It changed the rules, effective immediately, running from 26 June through 31 July 2026:

    • Composition fines for first-time offences detected during inspections went up from S$2,000 to S$3,000, with steeper fines for repeat or more serious breaches.
    • Stop-Work Orders now run a minimum of eight weeks, up from five.
    • Companies with the most serious lapses — ones resulting in fatal or serious accidents — can be barred from hiring new migrant workers for three months.

    Alongside this, MOM called for a nationwide voluntary Safety Time-Out: two weeks where employers were asked to pause, walk their own processes, talk to workers and supervisors about risk, and close any gaps they find — with specific attention to vehicle movement, individual worker lapses, and emergency response procedures.

    Why the "voluntary" part doesn't mean optional

    Here's the part that tends to get missed. The Safety Time-Out itself was voluntary. The enforcement measures around it are not. Inspections are continuing — arguably more intensively — for the full window, and every one of those inspections is happening under the new, harsher fine and shutdown structure.

    That distinction matters for how you should be reading this moment. Whether or not you paused for the Time-Out, if MOM walks onto your site before July 31 and finds a lapse, you're not looking at the penalties you might have budgeted for six months ago. You're looking at the new ones.

    What an 8-week stop-work order actually costs

    A composition fine is a number you can absorb. An 8-week stop-work order is a different category of problem entirely. For a kitchen, a workshop, or a logistics operation running on normal SME margins, two months of zero revenue — while fixed costs like rent, salaries, and supplier commitments keep running — isn't an inconvenience. It's the kind of event that decides whether a business is still open by the end of the year.

    That's the real cost of this enforcement window, and it's the one most SME owners aren't pricing in.

    What to actually do before July 31

    You don't need to overhaul your entire operation in a week. You need to close the gaps that would turn a routine inspection into a stop-work order:

    Before-July-31 checklist
    1. Walk your site like an inspector would. Not a glance — an actual walk-through against your last risk assessment, checking whether what's written down still matches what's happening on the floor.
    2. Check your highest-risk activities first. MOM specifically flagged vehicle-related activities and emergency response procedures. If you've got forklifts, delivery vehicles, or any moving equipment, start there.
    3. Confirm your emergency response is more than a poster on the wall. Do your supervisors actually know what to do in the first five minutes after an incident? Has anyone tested that, or is it assumed?
    4. Get a second set of eyes on it. The gaps that get a business into trouble are almost always the ones the business itself has stopped noticing, because they've become normal.
    The Safety Time-Out was voluntary. The fines and stop-work orders that follow it are not.

    If it's been a while since your last proper risk assessment — or if you've never had one done by someone with no stake in telling you everything's fine — this window is the reason to get it done now rather than after an inspection finds the gap for you.
