Hiring a Safety Coordinator vs outsourcing your WSH — a cost breakdown for Singapore SMEs.

Most business owners assume hiring someone is cheaper. When you add CPF, levies, leave entitlements, and the hidden costs nobody mentions, the numbers tell a very different story.

By OSS → 9 min read → WSH Strategy

The question every growing SME eventually asks

The WSH Act applies to every workplace in Singapore, regardless of size or sector. MOM regulations require it, your clients are starting to ask about it, and the risk of a site incident — and the liability that comes with it — is real. So you start looking at your options.

Option A: Hire a Safety Coordinator or Safety Officer full-time. Option B: Engage a WSH consultancy on a retainer. On the surface, Option A looks cheaper. A fresh coordinator might cost you S$2,800 to S$3,500 a month in basic salary. A fractional WSHO retainer from a firm like OSS runs S$650 to S$2,500 a month depending on scope.

But that comparison is wrong from the start, because basic salary is only part of what a hire actually costs you.

The real cost of an employee is not what you pay them. It is what it costs you to employ them — every month, whether they are on leave, sick, or underperforming.

The true cost of hiring a Safety Coordinator in Singapore

Let us build the full picture using a realistic mid-market Safety Coordinator earning S$3,200 per month basic salary. This is not a senior WSHO — this is a coordinator or executive level, the kind of person most SMEs would realistically hire for general safety administration.

1. CPF contributions (employer)

Under MOM’s CPF contribution rates, employers are required to contribute to their employees’ CPF accounts on top of the salary paid. For a Singapore Citizen or Permanent Resident aged 55 and below, the employer contribution rate is 17% of ordinary wages.

On a S$3,200 basic salary, that is S$544 per month in employer CPF that never appears in the employee’s take-home pay — but absolutely comes out of your pocket.

CPF Component Rate Monthly Amount
Employer CPF (on S$3,200 basic) 17% S$544
Employee CPF (deducted from salary) 20% S$640 (not your cost)
Your actual cash outflow (salary + employer CPF) S$3,744

Note: If you hire a Malaysian work pass holder, CPF does not apply — but you will pay the Skills Development Levy (SDL) and potentially a Foreign Worker Levy instead (see below).

2. Skills Development Levy (SDL)

All employers in Singapore must pay the SDL for every employee — local or foreign. The levy is 0.25% of gross monthly wages, subject to a minimum of S$2 and a maximum of S$11.25 per employee per month.

For our S$3,200 coordinator, that is S$8 per month. Small, but it adds up across a headcount.

3. Foreign Worker Levy (if applicable)

If your Safety Coordinator holds a Work Permit or S Pass, you are also liable for the Foreign Worker Levy. S Pass holders attract a levy of S$550 to S$650 per month depending on your sector’s Dependency Ratio Ceiling (DRC) tier. This is on top of salary — and it is non-negotiable.

Many SME owners overlook this entirely when budgeting for a foreign hire. A S$3,200 salary plus S$600 levy is already S$3,800 before CPF considerations even come into play for PR employees, or before any benefits.

4. Annual leave entitlement

Under the Employment Act, employees are entitled to paid annual leave starting at 7 days in the first year, scaling up to 14 days by year eight. At S$3,200 basic, each day of annual leave costs you approximately S$147. Over 14 days, that is S$2,058 per year in leave liability alone — leave your coordinator takes while you still need WSH coverage.

5. Sick leave and hospitalisation leave

Employees are entitled to up to 14 days outpatient sick leave and 60 days hospitalisation leave per year under the Employment Act (after three months of service). You are obligated to pay full salary during sick leave. Hospitalisation leave at full pay for up to 60 days represents a liability of up to S$9,230 — a risk you carry every year of employment.

6. Public holidays

Singapore has 11 gazetted public holidays per year. If a public holiday falls on a rest day, a substitute day is given. Your employee is entitled to these days at full pay. That is another S$1,617 in annual wage cost for time not worked.

7. Medical and insurance benefits

Most employers provide at minimum:

  • Group hospitalisation and surgical (H&S) insurance: approximately S$50–S$100 per employee per month
  • Work Injury Compensation (WIC) insurance: mandatory under WICA for all employees doing manual work or earning below S$2,600/month. If your coordinator does any site work or inspections, you likely need this. Premiums vary but budget S$30–S$80 per month.
  • Medical outpatient coverage or claims reimbursement: S$30–S$60 per month on average

Conservative estimate: S$110–S$240 per month in insurance and medical benefits.

8. Recruitment and onboarding cost

Hiring takes time and money. If you use a recruitment agency, expect to pay one to two months’ salary as a placement fee — that is S$3,200 to S$6,400 upfront. Even if you hire directly via JobStreet or LinkedIn, you are spending internal hours on screening, interviewing, and reference checks. Then there is onboarding: uniform, equipment, access cards, IT setup, and the first 1–3 months where productivity is below par.

Amortised over a 12-month employment period, recruitment and onboarding typically adds S$400–S$600 per month to the effective cost of a hire.

9. Training and certification

WSH roles require ongoing certification. BizSAFE training, first aid recertification, WSQ courses, and any sector-specific training (MEWP, confined space, hot work) all cost money. Budget at least S$800–S$1,500 per year in training costs, or S$70–S$125 per month.

10. Management overhead

A full-time employee needs to be managed. Performance reviews, HR administration, disciplinary processes, and the time your operations manager spends supervising a junior coordinator all have a cost. This is hard to quantify precisely, but most businesses estimate supervisor time at 10–15% of an employee’s working hours. At S$3,200 basic, that represents S$320–S$480 per month in management overhead.

What you get — and what you do not get — with a full-time hire

A full-time Safety Coordinator is physically present on site. For high-risk, high-activity operations — shipyards, construction sites, semiconductor fabs with constant permit-to-work activity — that presence matters enormously. If your operation genuinely requires someone on the ground every single day managing permits, supervising contractors, and running toolbox talks, a full-time hire makes sense.

But most Singapore SMEs — F&B chains, workshops, warehouses, light industrial firms — do not have that level of daily WSH activity. What they need is:

  • A MOM-registered WSHO to sign off on risk assessments and represent them to regulators
  • Monthly or fortnightly site inspections with a proper corrective action tracker
  • Toolbox talk frameworks and training their supervisors can run
  • Incident investigation capability when something goes wrong
  • Someone available on the phone when MOM calls

That is not a full-time job. That is a fractional engagement.

When a full-time hire genuinely makes sense

Outsourcing is not the right answer for every business. A full-time Safety Coordinator or Officer makes sense when:

  • Your operation runs active high-risk work every day — confined space entries, hot work, lifting operations, working at height — and you need dedicated permit-to-work oversight on the ground
  • You have a headcount above 200 and MOM regulations require a named, on-site WSHO under the WSH (Safety and Health Management System) Regulations
  • Your clients or main contractors require full-time dedicated safety personnel on site as a contractual condition
  • You are in a sector with mandatory WSH officer ratios such as shipbuilding, petrochemical, or construction above certain project values

Outside of these scenarios — and most Singapore SMEs in F&B, workshops, warehouses, and light marine fit outside them — you are paying full-time costs for part-time needs.

The resignation problem nobody talks about

There is one more cost that does not appear in any spreadsheet: what happens when your Safety Coordinator resigns.

Singapore’s safety job market is active. Experienced coordinators and officers know their value and move regularly. When yours leaves, you face a 1–3 month gap while you re-recruit, a fresh recruitment fee of S$3,200–S$6,400, and the risk that MOM asks during that gap whether your WSH programme is being properly managed. If you are required to have a named WSHO and that person just resigned, you have a compliance problem from day one of their departure.

With a retainer arrangement, none of that is your problem. The continuity is built in.

Making the decision

Before you decide, ask yourself three questions:

  • Do I need WSH coverage every day, or do I need it done properly every month? If it is the latter, you do not need a full-time hire.
  • What is my actual WSH activity level? If a typical week involves one toolbox talk, a handful of safety observations, and some documentation, that is three to four hours of work — not a full-time role.
  • What is the cost of getting this wrong? A MOM Stop-Work Order costs you far more than any retainer. An incident without proper documentation exposes you to WICA claims and potential prosecution under the WSH Act.

For most SMEs, a fractional WSHO retainer delivers better compliance outcomes, better expertise, and lower total cost than a full-time hire. The maths are clear. The risk profile is better. And unlike an employee, a good WSH consultancy is invested in keeping your programme genuinely compliant — because their reputation depends on it.
